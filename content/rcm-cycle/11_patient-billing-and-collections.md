## Patient responsibility is growing

High-deductible health plans mean patients now pay a large share of many bills. For many practices, patient payments are a significant part of revenue, and they're harder to collect than insurance payments: the longer a patient balance sits, the less likely it is to be paid.

## What the patient can be billed

The patient can be billed for:

- **Deductible, coinsurance and copay** (PR-1, PR-2, PR-3)
- **Non-covered services the patient agreed to pay**, ideally with a signed waiver or ABN (for Original Medicare)
- **Services denied for patient-side reasons**, when the plan or contract allows, such as coverage terminated and no other insurance
- **Self-pay services** (no insurance)

The patient generally **can't** be billed for:

- **Contractual adjustments (CO-45)** by an in-network provider
- **Denials with CO group codes**, which are the provider's liability (for example a missing authorization the provider should have obtained, when the contract holds the patient harmless)
- **Medicare cost-sharing for QMB patients** (see [Medicaid, QMB and spend-down](/insurance/medicaid-qmb-spend-down/))
- **Emergency and certain surprise out-of-network bills above in-network cost-sharing**, under the No Surprises Act

## Before the visit: estimates

- **Price estimates** using verified benefits help patients plan and improve collection.
- **Good faith estimates (GFE):** under the **No Surprises Act** (effective 2022), providers must give **uninsured or self-pay** patients a written estimate for scheduled services (within set timeframes) and on request. If the final bill is substantially higher than the estimate (currently $400 or more), the patient can use a federal dispute process.
- **Upfront collection:** copays and known balances at check-in; deposits for elective procedures.

## Statements

Patient statements usually go out on a **cycle**, for example every 30 days:

| Statement | Typical message |
|---|---|
| First statement | Balance due after insurance, with payment options |
| Second statement | Reminder |
| Third statement | Past due; offer of payment plan |
| Final notice | Account may be sent to a collection agency by a stated date |

Statements must be clear: date of service, provider, what insurance paid, what the patient owes and why, and how to pay. Confusing statements generate calls and slow payment.

## Payment options

- Online portal, card, bank transfer, phone
- **Payment plans**: monthly instalments under the practice's policy
- **Prompt-pay discounts** for self-pay patients, when policy allows

## Financial assistance and charity care

Non-profit hospitals must have a written **financial assistance policy (FAP)** under IRS section 501(r) and screen patients for eligibility before taking extraordinary collection actions. Many providers also screen self-pay patients for **Medicaid eligibility**, which can sometimes be granted retroactively for up to three months before application.

## Collections and bad debt

When a patient doesn't pay after the statement cycle and outreach:

1. The account is reviewed against policy (balance threshold, attempts made, assistance screening).
2. It's **written off to bad debt** in the provider's books.
3. It's **placed with a collection agency**, which collects for a percentage.

Rules to know:

- The **Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)** governs how third-party collectors contact patients.
- The three major credit bureaus no longer report medical debt under $500, paid medical debt, or medical debt less than a year old, and rules in this area continue to change, so follow current law and client policy.
- **Medicare bad debt:** hospitals and some other facilities can claim unpaid Medicare deductibles and coinsurance as allowable bad debt on their cost report, but only after reasonable collection effort, and with special rules for dual-eligible patients.

## Patient calls

Patient billing teams take calls like “Why do I owe this?” A good answer:

1. Verifies the caller's identity (HIPAA).
2. Explains what insurance paid and why the remainder is the patient's share, in plain words.
3. Checks for errors: was secondary insurance billed? Was the copay already paid at the desk?
4. Offers payment options.
5. Documents the call.

!!! warning "Check before you bill"
    Before a balance goes to the patient, confirm all insurance has processed. Billing a patient for an amount their secondary should cover causes complaints, refunds and sometimes regulatory problems.

!!! mistake "Auditor's red flag"
    Statements sent to the patient while a secondary claim is still open, or to QMB patients for Medicare cost-sharing. Both cause complaints, refunds and, for QMB, regulatory risk.

!!! interview "If an interviewer asks “When do you bill the patient?”"
    “After all insurance has processed. The patient is billed the patient responsibility shown on the remittance, such as deductible, coinsurance and copay, or non-covered services they agreed to pay. We don't bill contractual adjustments or provider-liability denials, and we don't bill QMB patients for Medicare cost-sharing.”
